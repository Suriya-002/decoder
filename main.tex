\begin{abstract}
Atom loss is a dominant error source in neutral-atom quantum processors, and a single
entangling gate can remove both of the atoms it acts on. The probability that a gate which
loses one atom loses both is a hardware parameter that materially changes predicted
error-correction performance: recent studies report loss thresholds varying by roughly fifty
percent across its range. That parameter is currently treated as a free input, because no
method exists to measure it without dedicated loss-resolving readout or additional
loss-detection circuitry. We show that it can be recovered in closed form from the measurement
record that a surface-code memory experiment already produces. When a gate removes one atom,
the stabilizer measurement associated with its partner takes a value fixed by two constants
that follow from the code structure rather than from calibration, and the mixture of those two
values determines the correlation directly. The resulting estimator requires no additional
hardware, no calibration experiment, and no trained model. We validate it against known answers
across three code families and three code distances, under a refined neutral-atom noise model
including biased errors on surviving atoms and a measured gate error budget, and we quantify
the shot cost as a function of the quality of the upstream loss-localization step.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{N}{eutral}-atom processors have become a leading platform for quantum error~\cite{bluvstein2024logicalprocessor}
correction, but they carry an error mechanism that superconducting~\cite{acharya2023scalingsurfacecode,acharya2024belowthreshold} and trapped-ion systems do
not share at comparable rates: atoms are physically lost from their traps. In recent
high-fidelity entangling-gate demonstrations, atom loss accounts for roughly half of the total
two-qubit gate error budget, at $0.061\%$ per gate against $0.035\%$ leakage, $0.024\%$
Pauli-$Z$, and $0.003\%$ Pauli-$X(Y)$~\cite{evered2026highfidelitygates}. Loss is not a Pauli
error. A lost qubit leaves the computational space entirely, so the stabilizers supported on it
become undefined rather than flipped, and the decoders and error models built for Pauli noise
do not describe it without modification.

A further feature distinguishes atom loss from the erasure channels usually studied. Rydberg
gates act on a pair of atoms brought into interaction range, and the mechanisms that eject one
atom, including anti-trapping in the Rydberg state and radiative decay to untrapped levels, can
eject both. The relevant quantity is
\begin{equation*}
  \eta = \Pr\!\left[\text{partner atom lost} \,\middle|\, \text{this atom lost in the same gate}\right],
\end{equation*}
the conditional probability that a gate which loses one atom loses both. This parameter is
already established in the literature: it appears as $p_c$ in
Ref.~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss} and under the present symbol in
Ref.~\cite{liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss}, with the same definition in each case.

It is also unmeasured. Both of those studies treat $\eta$ as a free input and sweep it across
its full range, and both report that the sweep matters. Reference~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}
finds the loss threshold moving from $3.2\%$ to $4\%$ as the correlation increases;
Reference~\cite{liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss} reports the corresponding threshold moving from
$5.15\%$ to $7.82\%$. Two independent studies therefore publish hardware performance predictions
that vary by roughly fifty percent depending on a parameter that neither can determine for any
particular device. Measuring it has so far required either loss-resolving readout, available in
metastable-qubit encodings through erasure
conversion~\cite{wu2022erasureconversion,ma2023midcircuiterasure}, or teleportation-based
loss-detection units inserted into the syndrome-extraction
circuit~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}. Both are additional hardware, and neither is present in
a standard surface-code memory experiment.

An objection presents itself immediately. Both studies find that correlated loss is
\emph{beneficial}, raising the threshold rather than lowering it, so it might appear that the
precise value is unimportant. The benefit, however, is a curve rather than a constant. A device
at $\eta = 0.3$ and a device at $\eta = 0.9$ sit at materially different points on the published
threshold predictions, with correspondingly different resource estimates and different answers
to whether correlated-loss-aware decoding is worth implementing at all. Those studies quantify
the payoff as a function of the parameter; neither determines which payoff a given device
receives. A threshold prediction that depends on an unmeasured quantity is a family of
predictions, and selecting among them requires a measurement.

This work provides one. We show that $\eta$ can be recovered in closed form from the raw
measurement record that a surface-code memory experiment already produces. The estimator rests
on two constants that are fixed by the code structure and the readout mechanism rather than by
calibration. When a data qubit is lost and its gate partner survives, the truncated stabilizers
on the lost qubit anticommute and the partner ancilla's outcome is unbiased. When the partner is
co-lost, that ancilla is absent at readout and cannot be bright, up to a false-bright rate
measurable by reading out an empty trap. The observed rate is a mixture of the two, and
inverting the mixture gives $\eta$ directly.

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A closed-form estimator for the gate-pair atom-loss correlation, built from two
        theory-pinned constants and one directly measurable hardware parameter, requiring no
        loss-resolving readout, no loss-detection circuitry, no dedicated calibration
        experiment, and no trained model.
  \item Validation against known answers rather than against reference implementations. Both
        constants are verified by forced injection over $9.6\times10^{6}$ samples and shown to
        be uniform across stabilizer degree, ancilla type, and gate substep, and the full
        pipeline is gated behind a suite of thirty known-answer checks.
  \item A quantitative account of why the detector representation standard in this literature
        cannot deliver this estimate. The parity of consecutive rounds is the operation that
        makes syndrome data informative for Pauli noise, and it is also the operation that
        destroys the loss signature on any stabilizer whose sign was randomly projected. We
        state this as a prediction, measure the resulting representation gap, and confirm that
        it also applies to the two-step construction developed for leakage
        detection~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection}.
  \item Demonstration across three code families and three code distances, with the gate
        schedule detected rather than assumed, including a color code whose layer count and
        stabilizer sign structure both differ from the surface code.
  \item Characterization of the shot cost as a function of the quality of the upstream
        loss-localization step, showing that a localizer wrong three times in four costs a
        factor of roughly three in precision, so the method does not require a strong loss
        decoder.
  \item An explicit negative result. Supplying the estimated correlation to a decoder that
        discounts syndrome bits from co-lost ancillas does not reduce the logical error rate,
        for a reason internal to the estimator's own physics, and we report this alongside the
        control experiment that establishes the null is real.
\end{enumerate}

We note at the outset what is not claimed. Using raw stabilizer outcomes in place of detector
differences is not a novel input representation; it is established for leakage detection in
superconducting devices~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection} and has
been applied to atom loss by a learned decoder that consumes raw outcomes and detectors as
separate features~\cite{wang2026aiqubitloss}. The contribution here is the estimand and the
closed form, together with the quantified representation gap and the argument that accounts for
it.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Atom Loss and Its Correction}
\label{sec:rel-loss}

Atom loss in a neutral-atom processor has several physical origins~\cite{morgado2021rydbergreview}. During a Rydberg-mediated
entangling gate the excited state is anti-trapped in a conventional optical tweezer, so an atom
that remains in the Rydberg state at the end of the pulse can be ejected; radiative decay from
the Rydberg manifold can populate levels that the trap does not confine; and background-gas
collisions and imperfect recapture during imaging contribute additional loss outside the gate.
Reference~\cite{evered2026highfidelitygates} reports a per-atom per-gate loss probability of
$0.054\%$, and decomposes the two-qubit gate error into $0.061\%$ loss, $0.035\%$ leakage,
$0.024\%$ Pauli-$Z$, and $0.003\%$ Pauli-$X(Y)$, making loss the single largest component.

Loss is qualitatively different from a Pauli error~\cite{terhal2015qecreview}. A Pauli error leaves the qubit in the
computational space and flips the stabilizers that anticommute with it, producing a detector
event that a matching decoder can interpret. A lost qubit leaves the space entirely: the
stabilizers supported on it are no longer well defined, and the corresponding measurement
outcomes become uninformative in a way that persists for the rest of the experiment rather than
manifesting as a single flip. Decoders and error models constructed for Pauli noise therefore do
not describe loss without modification.

The standard remedy is to convert loss into erasure. If the location of a lost qubit is known,
the error becomes a Pauli error of known support and unknown type, which is substantially easier
to correct than an error of unknown location: Ref.~\cite{delfosse2020erasuredecoding} gives a
linear-time maximum-likelihood decoder for surface codes over the erasure channel, and the loss
thresholds of Refs.~\cite{stace2009lossthresholds,stace2010degeneracyloss} are obtained by
merging the stabilizers that touched a lost qubit into superstabilizers supported on the
surviving qubits, at the cost of reduced effective code distance. This construction, in various
forms, underlies most subsequent loss-tolerant decoding; Ref.~\cite{baranes2026lossdetection,baranes2025architecture}
develops the corresponding superchecks in a fault-tolerant algorithmic setting.

All of these approaches presuppose that loss locations are known, which requires an additional
mechanism. Three exist. Erasure conversion encodes the qubit in a metastable manifold so that
the dominant error decays to a state distinguishable from both computational levels, allowing
mid-circuit detection~\cite{wu2022erasureconversion}, an approach demonstrated in Rydberg
simulators~\cite{scholl2023erasurerydberg} and in gate
experiments~\cite{ma2023midcircuiterasure}. Loss-detection units insert teleportation-based
circuitry into the syndrome-extraction cycle that heralds whether an atom is
present~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}. Finally, a decoder can attempt to infer loss locations
from the syndrome record itself, which we discuss in Sec.~\ref{sec:rel-syndrome}. The first two
require hardware not present in a standard surface-code memory experiment; the third does not,
and is the regime this work operates in. A related line of work locates Rydberg decay events within leakage-reduction circuits~\cite{yu2026rydbergdecayswaplrc}.

\subsection{Correlated Atom Loss}
\label{sec:rel-correlated}

Because a Rydberg gate acts on two atoms brought into interaction range, and because the
mechanisms that eject one atom can act on both, loss at a gate is not necessarily an independent
single-atom event. This possibility was noted and explicitly deferred in
Ref.~\cite{perrin2025qecatomloss}, which assigns each gate a loss probability $p_l$ and treats
the simultaneous loss of both atoms as the coincidence of two independent events with
probability $p_l^2$, while observing that genuinely correlated loss could occur in experiment and
leaving its investigation to future work.

That investigation is Ref.~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}, which introduces a second parameter
quantifying the conditional probability of losing the second atom given that the first was lost
in the same gate. Their decoder constructs a loss graph from heralds supplied by
teleportation-based loss-detection units and updates per-edge loss posteriors within it, and
they report that correlated loss is a resource rather than a liability: relative to a decoder
assuming independent loss, they obtain up to an order-of-magnitude reduction in logical error
probability and a loss threshold rising from $3.2\%$ to $4\%$. The correlation parameter itself
enters as an a priori input and is swept across its full range from $0$ to $1$.

Reference~\cite{liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss} reaches a compatible conclusion by a different
route. Its appendix on correlated atom loss adopts the parameterization in which, for an error
rate $p$ and correlated-loss contribution $\eta$, each atom is lost independently with
probability $p(1-\eta)/2$ and together with its partner with probability $p\eta/2$, and shows
that correlated loss admits the same effective distance as Pauli errors. The reported threshold
rises from $5.15\%$ to $7.82\%$ as $\eta$ increases from $0$ to $1$. As in
Ref.~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}, $\eta$ is a swept simulation input; the decoding
constructions in that work additionally assume loss-resolving readout.

Related work from the same program addresses adjacent aspects of the loss channel.
Reference~\cite{pecorari2026lossbiased} proposes converting spurious Rydberg excitations into
atom loss by mid-circuit ionization, biasing the noise toward a detectable channel, and argues
that loss-aware decoding then approaches optimal erasure scaling.
Reference~\cite{jandura2026stabilizermeasurements} treats the gate-level protocol design that
determines how leakage and decay propagate into the stabilizer measurement.

The common structure across this literature is that the correlation parameter is defined,
varied, and shown to matter, but is never inferred from data. Every quantitative claim about its
consequences is therefore conditional on a value that no reported method can supply for a
specific device.

\subsection{Extracting Loss Information from the Syndrome Record}
\label{sec:rel-syndrome}

The observation that the raw stabilizer measurement record carries information that the detector
record does not is established, and predates this work by several years. It arose for leakage
rather than loss. Reference~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity} detects leakage in a
superconducting device directly from the repeated parity record, exploiting the fact that a
leaked ancilla produces a persistent fixed readout outcome because the measurement cannot
distinguish the leaked state from one of the computational levels, and defines a two-step
syndrome formed from measurements two rounds apart precisely because the ordinary
consecutive-round detector destroys that signature.
Reference~\cite{varbanov2020leakagedetection} extends the approach to a transmon surface code
using hidden Markov models over the same record.

For atom loss specifically, Ref.~\cite{wang2026aiqubitloss} decodes loss events directly from
the stabilizer record without loss-detection units or specialized atomic species, on the stated
grounds that avoiding such hardware is desirable. That work exploits the same physical signature
this paper relies on: a lost data qubit causes overlapping stabilizers to anticommute, so the
affected outcomes collapse to either sign with equal probability, producing temporally
correlated flickering rather than an isolated flip. It supplies a spatio-temporal graph neural
network with both the binary measurement outcome and the detector value as separate input
features, and reports a recall of $0.654$ at precision $0.845$ over ten rounds at distance five. That work sits within a broader line of learned decoders for topological codes~\cite{torlai2017neuraldecoder,varsamopoulos2018feedforward,baireuther2018mlassisted,chamberland2018deepneuraldecoders,bausch2024alphaqubit}.

We state plainly that the choice to use raw stabilizer outcomes rather than detector differences
for loss inference is therefore not novel to this work, and we do not claim it. Three
differences nevertheless separate what follows from Ref.~\cite{wang2026aiqubitloss}. First, the
estimand differs: that work localizes individual loss events in space and time, while this work
estimates a parameter of the loss model. Second, the method differs: that work trains a
supervised network whose performance depends on its training distribution, while
Eq.~\eqref{eq:estimator} is closed-form with constants fixed by the code structure. Third, the
loss model differs: that work adopts a round-level independent loss channel with no correlation
parameter, so the quantity estimated here does not appear in it. The relationship is
complementary rather than competitive, and indeed a localizer of the kind that work provides is
one natural front end for the estimator, a case we evaluate quantitatively in
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-localizer}.

What this work adds to the representational question is quantitative rather than conceptual. The
prior literature makes the representational choice; it does not measure what the choice is worth,
and it does not give the argument that determines when the detector record fails. We supply both,
and show in particular that the two-step construction of
Refs.~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection} does not recover the
atom-loss signature, for a reason that follows directly from the argument in
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory-detector}.

\subsection{Estimating Error-Model Parameters from Syndrome Data}
\label{sec:rel-estimation}

Inferring error-model parameters from syndrome extraction data is an established program with
its own methodology. Reference~\cite{spitz2018adaptiveweight} introduced the pairwise estimator
that recovers effective error probabilities from correlations between detector events, allowing
matching weights to be adapted to measured noise rather than assumed.
Reference~\cite{blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels} places this on a general footing, deriving a
framework for estimating probabilities of individual and aggregated detector-error-model events
from multi-cycle syndrome data and recovering the earlier pairwise estimator as a special case.
A parallel line estimates Pauli channels describing syndrome-extraction noise, and
Ref.~\cite{hockings2025aces} estimates circuit eigenvalues for syndrome-extraction circuits from
raw measurement data at scale.

Two features of this literature bear on the present work. First, its target is Pauli and
measurement error mechanisms, for which the detector representation is not merely adequate but
correct: those mechanisms manifest as changes in stabilizer values, and the detector
transformation isolates exactly those changes while discarding the gauge freedom that carries no
information. Second, and consequently, the entire program is built on detector histories. Both
Ref.~\cite{spitz2018adaptiveweight} and Ref.~\cite{blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels} transform
raw syndrome records into detector histories by taking the parity of consecutive rounds before
any estimation is performed.

That transformation is where atom loss is lost. Section~\ref{sec:theory-detector} shows that a
lost atom pins a measurement outcome to a fixed value regardless of the stabilizer's gauge, so
the operation that removes the gauge also removes the signature, on any stabilizer whose sign was
randomly projected. Section~\ref{sec:res-representation} measures the resulting gap. These
methods are therefore not competitors that this work outperforms on a shared task; they are
methods whose input representation excludes the error class considered here, and the estimator
below should be understood as addressing a class they do not reach rather than as an improvement
on their performance within their own domain.

This work occupies that position. It does not improve on the decoders of

what the alternative representation costs.



\section{Estimating the Loss Correlation from Syndrome Measurements}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Loss Model and the Correlation Parameter}
\label{sec:theory-model}

We model atom loss at the level of individual two-qubit gates. Following the parameterization
used in the neutral-atom error-correction
literature~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss,liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss}, define
\begin{equation}
  \eta \;=\; \Pr\!\left[\text{partner atom lost} \,\middle|\, \text{this atom lost in the same CZ}\right],
  \label{eq:eta-def}
\end{equation}
the conditional probability that a gate which loses one atom loses both. For a gate acting on
atoms $(a,b)$ with per-atom loss probability $p_g$,
\begin{align}
  \Pr[\text{both lost}] &= \eta\,p_g, \label{eq:both}\\
  \Pr[\text{only } a] = \Pr[\text{only } b] &= (1-\eta)\,p_g. \label{eq:one}
\end{align}
The per-atom marginal is $p_g$ independently of $\eta$. This matching is essential: without it,
varying $\eta$ would change the total loss budget simultaneously with the correlation, and no
observable could be attributed to either alone.

Equations~\eqref{eq:both}--\eqref{eq:one} are equivalent to the parameterization of
Ref.~\cite{liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss}, in which each atom is lost independently with
probability $p(1-\eta)/2$ and together with its partner with probability $p\eta/2$, under
$p = 2p_g$, and to the parameter $p_c$ of Ref.~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}.

In a surface-code syndrome-extraction circuit every two-qubit gate acts between one data qubit
and one ancilla; there are no data--data entangling gates. The partner of a lost data qubit in a
correlated-loss event is therefore always an ancilla. Ancillas are reset and measured every
round, so ancilla loss is transient, while data loss persists for the remainder of the
experiment. The estimator below exploits this asymmetry.

\subsection{Two Theory-Pinned Anchors}
\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_mechanism.png}
\caption{Mechanism of the estimator. A two-qubit gate acts between a data qubit and an ancilla.
(a) When only the data qubit is lost, the stabilizers supported on it are truncated and
anticommute, so the surviving partner ancilla's raw outcome is unbiased. (b) When both atoms are
lost, the ancilla is absent at readout and cannot be bright, up to the false-bright rate
$\epsilon$. The observed rate is a mixture of the two cases weighted by $\eta$, and inverting the
mixture recovers $\eta$ directly. Neither constant is fitted.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}
\label{sec:theory-anchors}

Consider a data qubit lost during a gate, and let $a$ be the ancilla it was coupled to at that
gate. Let $m_a$ denote the raw measurement outcome of that ancilla in the loss round.

If the partner ancilla survives, the loss of the data qubit truncates the stabilizers supported
on it. The truncated $X$-type and $Z$-type stabilizers anticommute, so the measured outcome
collapses to $\pm1$ with equal probability:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[m_a = 0 \mid \text{partner alive}] = \tfrac{1}{2}.
  \label{eq:anchor1}
\end{equation}

If the partner ancilla is co-lost, it is absent at readout and cannot be bright. Atom-array
readout detects population in the excited state, so an empty trap is indistinguishable from the
ground state, up to a false-bright rate $\epsilon$ arising from dark counts or stray scatter:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr[m_a = 0 \mid \text{partner co-lost}] = 1-\epsilon.
  \label{eq:anchor2}
\end{equation}

Neither constant is fitted. Equation~\eqref{eq:anchor1} follows from the code structure and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:anchor2} from the readout mechanism; $\epsilon$ is measured directly on hardware
by reading out a trap known to be empty.

\subsection{The Estimator}
\label{sec:theory-estimator}

A fraction $\eta$ of loss events are co-lost, so the observed rate is a mixture of
\eqref{eq:anchor1} and \eqref{eq:anchor2}. Inverting the mixture gives
\begin{equation}
  \hat\eta \;=\; \frac{\Pr[m_a=0] - \tfrac{1}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon},
  \label{eq:estimator}
\end{equation}
with the binomial confidence interval on $\Pr[m_a=0]$ propagating through the linear map.
Equation~\eqref{eq:estimator} contains no fitted parameters and requires no loss-detection
unit, no loss-resolving readout, and no dedicated calibration experiment. It requires only that
loss events be localized well enough to identify which ancilla to read; we quantify the
sensitivity to that localization in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-localizer}.

\subsection{Why the Detector Representation Is Blind to Loss}
\label{sec:theory-detector}

Decoders and syndrome-statistics estimators do not consume raw measurement outcomes. They
consume detectors, the parity of consecutive rounds,
\begin{equation}
  d_a(r) = m_a(r) \oplus m_a(r-1).
  \label{eq:detector}
\end{equation}
This transformation is what makes the syndrome record informative for Pauli noise: the absolute
value of a stabilizer is set by a random projection in the first round and carries no
information, so only changes are meaningful.

Equation~\eqref{eq:detector} predicts that the same transformation destroys the loss signature.
A dead ancilla gives $m_a(r) = 0$ deterministically, so $d_a(r) = m_a(r-1)$, the previous
round's raw value. Two cases follow. If the stabilizer's sign is deterministic in the memory
basis, then $m_a(r-1) = 0$ and the detector reads $0$, distinguishable from the value $1/2$
expected of a surviving truncated ancilla. If the stabilizer's sign was randomly projected,
then $m_a(r-1)$ is an unbiased bit and the detector is a coin flip, indistinguishable from a
surviving truncated ancilla.

Half of the ancillas in a surface-code memory experiment measure randomly projected
stabilizers. On that half the detector representation carries no information about whether the
partner atom was co-lost, while the raw outcome reads $0$ in both cases. The prediction is
therefore that co-loss discrimination is near-total in the raw record for both ancilla types,
strong in the detector record for the deterministic type, and absent in the detector record for
the projected type. Section~\ref{sec:res-representation} tests this, including against the
two-step construction $s_a(r) = m_a(r)\oplus m_a(r-2)$ introduced for leakage
detection~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection}, which references a
round that is itself randomly projected and is therefore predicted to be blind as well.

The argument depends only on whether a stabilizer's sign is randomly projected, not on the
code. Which ancilla type is blind is basis- and code-dependent; that the projected type is
blind is not.

\subsection{Systematic Corrections}
\label{sec:theory-rules}

Equation~\eqref{eq:anchor1} is a steady-state statement, and two situations violate it.

At the start of the experiment the data register is in a product state, so a truncated
stabilizer in the memory basis remains deterministic rather than randomly projected and reads
$0$ with high probability. Losses occurring in the first round are therefore excluded.

Data loss is permanent, so a stabilizer truncated in round $r$ remains truncated in all later
rounds. Once the first truncated outcome is recorded, the surviving qubits are projected and
the stabilizer reads deterministically thereafter, so only the first truncated round satisfies
\eqref{eq:anchor1}. Restricting to first-truncation events removes the resulting bias.
Section~\ref{sec:res-estimator} shows that at physical loss rates the first-round exclusion
alone is sufficient, and the persistence correction matters only well above hardware loss
rates.


\section{Simulation Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Circuits and Noise}
\label{sec:methods-circuit}

All simulations use rotated surface-code memory experiments generated by
Stim~\cite{gidney2021stim}. The layout, syndrome-extraction schedule, and detector definitions
are taken directly from Stim's generated circuits rather than constructed by hand, so that the
hook-error-avoiding gate ordering is the standard one~\cite{tomita2014lowdistancesurfacecodes}.
Unless stated otherwise the configuration is distance $d=5$ with $T=12$ rounds and uniform
circuit-level depolarizing noise at $p=2\times10^{-3}$ applied after Clifford gates, before each
round on data qubits, before measurement, and after reset. At $d=5$ this gives $25$ data qubits
and $24$ ancillas ($12$ of each type), with $80$ two-qubit gates per round in four substeps of
$20$; each qubit participates in at most one gate per substep. We verify that all $80$ gates act
between one data qubit and one ancilla, spanning $80$ distinct Tanner edges with no violations.

Unless stated otherwise we take $p_g = 5.4\times10^{-4}$, the per-atom per-gate loss probability
reported in Ref.~\cite{evered2026highfidelitygates}. We confirm the marginal matching of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:both}--\eqref{eq:one} numerically: at $\eta = 0.00,\,0.25,\,0.50,\,1.00$ the
measured per-atom loss rate is $0.00995,\,0.00998,\,0.01004,\,0.00992$ against a target of
$0.01$, while the co-lost atom fraction tracks $\eta$ exactly.

We also implement the round-level independent loss model of Ref.~\cite{wang2026aiqubitloss}, in
which each qubit is removed with a fixed probability in each round. This is a distinct loss
mechanism rather than the $\eta \to 0$ limit of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:both}--\eqref{eq:one}, and we use
it only as an external reference point.

\subsection{Loss Injection}
\label{sec:methods-injection}

Atom loss is not a Pauli channel and cannot be injected as one. At the moment of loss we apply a
single-qubit depolarizing channel of strength $3/4$ to the affected qubit; the uniform Pauli
twirl is the completely depolarizing channel, so the atom is traced out and replaced by the
maximally mixed state. Thereafter, every subsequent operation on that qubit is removed, so a
lost atom performs no entangling gate on a surviving partner; the lost qubit's readout is forced
to $0$ by inserting a reset immediately before the measurement, in the basis matching that
measurement; ancillas are restored by their own mid-circuit reset each round while data qubits
are not; and measurements are never deleted, so that the measurement record retains a fixed
length and all detector record offsets remain valid.

The last point is a correctness requirement rather than a modeling choice. Deleting a
measurement shifts every downstream record offset and silently corrupts all subsequent
detectors. We enforce it with an invariant requiring that measurement and detector counts be
independent of the loss configuration, in both memory bases.

\subsection{Known-Answer Validation}
\label{sec:methods-checks}

Because the quantities in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} are fixed by theory, the simulation can be
checked against known answers rather than against itself. We maintain a suite of $30$ such
checks, all of which must pass before any result is reported. They cover the noiseless circuit
producing zero detector events; the no-loss rebuild reproducing the generated circuit exactly;
the data--ancilla structure of every two-qubit gate; a lost data qubit flickering exactly its
Tanner-partner ancillas and nothing else; the $\eta$-independence of the loss marginals; the
invariance of measurement and detector counts under loss in both bases; byte-level agreement
between an audited reference circuit builder and the faster builder used for production runs,
over $200$ random loss configurations in each basis; both anchors of
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory-anchors} at $\epsilon = 0$ and $\epsilon = 0.02$; recovery of planted
detector correlations by the baseline of Sec.~\ref{sec:methods-baselines}; and the generality
results of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-generality}.

Two design rules proved important. Checks use forced injection wherever a deterministic known
answer exists, rather than sampling; an early sampled version of the anchor check included
anomalous first-round events and sat at the edge of its tolerance. Checks also assert ratios
rather than absolute tolerances near the noise floor, since a check that passes intermittently
provides no information. The suite is reproduced across two platforms and two Python versions.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:methods-baselines}

We compare against the detector-record estimators standard in this
literature~\cite{spitz2018adaptiveweight,blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels}, implementing the
pairwise estimator in the form given by Blume-Kohout and
Young~\cite{blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels},
\begin{equation}
  p_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\langle z_i\rangle\langle z_j\rangle}{\langle z_i z_j\rangle}},
  \qquad z_i = (-1)^{d_i},
  \label{eq:pij}
\end{equation}
and validating it against planted correlations ($p_{12} = 0.05, 0.12, 0.03$ recovered as
$0.0504$, $0.1197$, $0.0300$; $0$ recovered as $-0.0005$). We additionally implement the
two-step syndrome of Refs.~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection}. All
three representations are evaluated on the same co-loss discrimination task, split by ancilla
type.

\subsection{Refined Neutral-Atom Physics}
\label{sec:methods-realism}

To test robustness beyond the gate-cancellation loss model we add two effects reported in the
neutral-atom literature. Following Ref.~\cite{perrin2025qecatomloss}, when exactly one atom of a
gate pair is lost the surviving partner suffers a maximally biased $Z$ error, implemented with
probability $1/2$. We also apply the residual Pauli components of the measured gate error budget
of Ref.~\cite{evered2026highfidelitygates}, $0.024\%$ Pauli-$Z$ and $0.003\%$ Pauli-$X(Y)$, to
every surviving gate; the loss and leakage components are carried by the loss channel itself.
The false-bright rate $\epsilon$ is swept from $0$ to $0.05$.

\subsection{Code-General Parsing}
\label{sec:methods-generality}

The rotated surface code has four gate layers per round; other codes do not. To apply the
estimator beyond a single code we detect the gate schedule rather than assume it: the circuit's
two-qubit gate layers are parsed, the repetition period identified, and each layer decomposed
into data--ancilla pairs. This requires only that each qubit participate in at most one gate per
layer and that every gate act between one data qubit and one ancilla, which we verify per code.
We report results for the rotated surface code at $d=3,5,7$; the unrotated surface
code~\cite{dennis2002topologicalmemory,fowler2012surfacecodes} at $d=5$, which has a different
stabilizer geometry but also four layers per round; and the color
code~\cite{bombin2006colorcodes} at $d=5$, a different code family with six layers per round.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Correlation Is Weakly Informative for Loss Detection}
\label{sec:res-ceiling}

Before estimating $\eta$ it is worth asking whether a decoder needs to know it in order to
\emph{find} losses. It does not. We computed the Bayes-optimal discrimination between a lost and
a surviving data qubit, as a likelihood-ratio test over the exact empirical distribution of
detector patterns on the victim's Tanner-partner ancillas. No architecture can exceed these
values on the same observation window.

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} reports the optimal AUC under uncorrelated ($\eta=0$) and fully
correlated ($\eta=1$) loss. The difference is at most $0.034$, it changes sign depending on
whether the final destructive readout falls inside the window, and it vanishes entirely once a
lost qubit has had two rounds in which to produce repeated syndrome flicker. Correlation is
therefore not a useful prior for loss localization.

The same table contains the result that makes estimation possible. Discriminating $\eta=1$ data
from $\eta=0$ data, rather than lost qubits from surviving ones, gives an AUC of $0.72$ to
$0.75$, stable across basis and window. The correlation is weakly informative about \emph{where}
losses are and strongly informative about \emph{itself}.

\subsection{Representation Dependence of the Co-Loss Signal}
\label{sec:res-representation}

Section~\ref{sec:theory-detector} predicts that the detector transformation destroys the
co-loss signature on randomly projected stabilizers while the raw record preserves it
everywhere. Table~\ref{tab:representation} confirms this. We compare three representations of
the same partner-ancilla observation on the same events: the raw outcome $m_a(r)$, the
one-step detector $m_a(r)\oplus m_a(r-1)$ consumed by matching decoders and by the estimators
of Refs.~\cite{spitz2018adaptiveweight,blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels}, and the two-step
syndrome $m_a(r)\oplus m_a(r-2)$ of
Refs.~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection}.

On the deterministic-sign ancilla type all three representations discriminate. On the randomly
projected type the raw record retains a gap of $0.49$ while both parity-based representations
collapse to $0.003$ and $0.008$, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The two-step
construction, designed for exactly this class of problem in superconducting devices, is blind
here for the reason given in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory-detector}: the round it differences against
is itself randomly projected.

\subsection{Robustness Under Refined Neutral-Atom Noise}
\label{sec:res-realism}

The anchors are unaffected by the refined loss physics of Sec.~\ref{sec:methods-realism}.
Table~\ref{tab:realism} adds, cumulatively, the maximally biased $Z$ error on the surviving
partner of a lossy gate, the residual Pauli components of the measured gate error budget, and a
false-bright rate. The first anchor moves by at most $0.0011$ and the second tracks $1-\epsilon$
exactly.

The insensitivity to the survivor bias has a simple cause. That error acts on the atom that
\emph{remains} after a one-sided loss, whereas the estimator reads the ancilla belonging to the
atom that was \emph{lost}. A $Z$ error on a surviving data qubit cannot change whether a dead
ancilla reads zero.

End-to-end recovery is likewise unchanged. At $\eta=0.5$ with high statistics the refined model
gives $\hat\eta = 0.4979 \pm 0.0125$ against $0.4931 \pm 0.0124$ for the plain model,
statistically identical. Sweeping the false-bright rate from $0$ to $0.05$ leaves every point
of the $\eta$ recovery inside its confidence interval.

\subsection{Generality Across Codes and Distances}
\label{sec:res-generality}

The argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:theory-detector} depends only on whether a stabilizer's sign is
randomly projected, and the experiments bear that out. Forcing a single ancilla dead at a bulk
round, the raw outcome reads $0$ with probability $1.0000$ on every code and distance tested,
while its detector fires with probability $0$ on the deterministic type and $0.5$ on the
projected type.

The color code is the sharpest test because it inverts the labels. In a memory-$Z$ surface-code
experiment the $Z$-type ancillas are deterministic, with steady-state mean outcome $0.000$, and
the $X$-type are projected, at $0.499$. In the color code the $Z$-type ancillas measure a
projected stabilizer, steady-state mean $0.498$, and their detectors are correspondingly blind.
Which ancilla type is blind depends on the code and basis; that the projected type is blind does
not.

code has six gate layers per round rather than four, and the unrotated surface code has a
different stabilizer geometry at the same layer count; in both cases the anchors hold and

\subsection{Effect of Knowing \texorpdfstring{$\eta$}{eta} on Decoding}
\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig4_impact.png}
\caption{Logical error rate under four decoder belief models at $p_g = 8\times10^{-3}$,
$\eta = 1$, $5500$ shots. Error bars are $95\%$ confidence intervals. The control gap between the
ignorant and data-aware arms establishes that the decoder responds strongly to loss information,
so the null between the data-aware and $\eta$-informed arms is a real null. The oracle arm, given
perfect knowledge of every dead ancilla, coincides exactly with the $\eta$-informed arm.}
\label{fig:impact}
\end{figure}
\label{sec:res-impact}

A natural question is whether supplying $\eta$ to a decoder improves the logical error rate. For
the most direct strategy available without loss heralds, it does not.

We decode the same lossy syndrome data four ways, differing only in what the decoder is told
about losses. Because a lossy circuit has non-deterministic detectors and a non-deterministic
logical observable, we sample from the true lossy circuit but decode with the clean detector
error model, modifying edge weights per shot to encode the decoder's beliefs. Decoding uses minimum-weight perfect matching~\cite{higgott2022pymatching,higgott2025sparseblossom}. Freeing an edge
means setting its probability to $1/2$. We verified that rebuilding the model with no
modification reproduces the baseline logical error rate exactly.

Table~\ref{tab:impact} gives the result. The control is decisive: a decoder told nothing about
losses has a logical error rate of $0.346$, while one told the data-qubit loss locations
achieves $0.219$, an improvement of $0.127 \pm 0.017$. The machinery therefore responds strongly
to loss information, and a null result between the remaining arms is a real null rather than an
insensitive measurement.

Additionally telling the decoder which ancillas were co-lost, so that it discounts their
corrupted syndrome bits, does not help: $0.235$ against $0.219$, a difference of $-0.015 \pm
0.016$ that is consistently negative across runs and machines but not individually significant.
This is not a limitation of the estimate's quality. An oracle arm given perfect knowledge of
every dead ancilla returns exactly the same logical error rate as the $\eta$-informed arm, since
at $\eta=1$ the informed belief coincides with the truth.

The reason connects directly to Sec.~\ref{sec:theory-anchors}. Discounting a co-lost ancilla
treats its reading as erased. But a dead ancilla reads zero \emph{deterministically}, which is
precisely the fact the estimator exploits, and its detector still constrains the previous
round's value. Erasing that reading discards more information than the correction recovers.

This tests one strategy, not the parameter's value in general. Ref.~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}
uses the same correlation inside a loss graph built from teleportation-based loss-detection unit
heralds and reports up to an order-of-magnitude reduction in logical error probability, together
with a loss threshold increase from $3.2\%$ to $4\%$. That approach requires hardware this work
does not assume. Integrating an estimated $\eta$ into a herald-based decoder of that kind is
left to future work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Estimator Supplies}
\label{sec:disc-purpose}

The correlation parameter is already established in the neutral-atom error-correction
literature, and it already carries quantitative consequences. Reference~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss}
sweeps it from $0$ to $1$ and reports the loss threshold moving from $3.2\%$ to $4\%$;
Reference~\cite{liu2026optimaldistanceatomloss} sweeps the same quantity and reports a threshold
moving from $5.15\%$ to $7.82\%$. Both treat it as a free input, because measuring it has
required either loss-resolving readout or dedicated loss-detection circuitry.

Equation~\eqref{eq:estimator} closes that gap. A device running a standard surface-code memory
experiment already produces the data the estimator needs, so $\eta$ can be reported alongside
gate fidelity and per-atom loss rate as a routine characterization number rather than assumed.
The practical consequence is that a platform can determine, from data it already collects,
which point on the published threshold curves it actually occupies, and therefore whether
correlated-loss-aware decoding is worth building for that hardware.

We emphasize that the benefit of correlated loss reported in those works does not make the
measurement unnecessary. The benefit is a curve, not a constant: $\eta = 0.3$ and $\eta = 0.9$
imply materially different thresholds and resource estimates. Those studies quantify the payoff;
neither determines which payoff a given device receives.

\subsection{Relation to Syndrome-Statistics Estimation}
\label{sec:disc-relation}

Estimating error-model parameters from syndrome data is an established
program~\cite{spitz2018adaptiveweight,blumekohout2025detectorerrormodels}. That program is built
on the detector record, which is the correct representation for the Pauli and measurement error
mechanisms it targets. Section~\ref{sec:res-representation} shows that the same representation
is blind to correlated atom loss on the randomly projected half of the stabilizer group, and
Section~\ref{sec:theory-detector} explains why: loss pins a measurement outcome to zero
regardless of the gauge that the detector transformation is designed to quotient away.

The relationship is therefore complementary rather than competitive. Detector-based estimators
remain the appropriate tool for the error classes they were built for. Atom loss requires a
different observable from the same experiment, and the estimator here is one instance of what
that observable makes available.

\subsection{Reconciling the Decoding Result}
\label{sec:disc-decoding}

Section~\ref{sec:res-impact} finds no decoding benefit from supplying $\eta$, while
Reference~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss} reports up to an order-of-magnitude reduction in
logical error probability from exploiting the same correlation. These are not in conflict, and
the difference is instructive.

Their decoder consumes heralds from teleportation-based loss-detection units, which identify
where atoms are missing, and uses the correlation to update loss probabilities within a loss
graph constructed from those heralds. The strategy tested here has no heralds available by
construction, since the premise of this work is that no such hardware is assumed, and so it uses
the correlation in the only way available without them: to discount syndrome bits from ancillas
believed to be co-lost.

That strategy fails for a reason that is internal to this paper's own physics. A dead ancilla
reads zero deterministically, which is exactly the fact Eq.~\eqref{eq:anchor2} rests on, and its
detector still constrains the previous round's outcome. Treating that reading as erased discards
information rather than correcting for it. The oracle arm confirms the diagnosis: perfect
knowledge of every dead ancilla performs identically to the $\eta$-informed arm, so the null is
a property of the strategy and not of the estimate.

The conclusion we draw is narrow and deliberately so. Knowing $\eta$ does not improve
detector-discounting decoding without heralds. Whether an estimated $\eta$ improves a
herald-based decoder of the kind in Reference~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss} is a separate
question requiring superstabilizer machinery this work does not implement.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Several limitations bound the scope of these results.

All experiments are memory experiments in a single basis at a time. Logical circuits involving
transversal gates, lattice surgery, or code deformation are not tested, and the anchors of
Section~\ref{sec:theory-anchors} are derived for the memory setting.

The codes tested are Calderbank-Shor-Steane codes with a data--ancilla gate structure and at most
one gate per qubit per layer. This covers the surface and color code families reported here but
excludes codes whose syndrome-extraction circuits violate those assumptions.

The noise model is circuit-level depolarizing noise together with the refined loss physics of
Section~\ref{sec:methods-realism}. It does not include crosstalk, atom transport or shuttling
loss between rounds, or correlated non-Markovian effects. No hardware data is used anywhere in
this work, and the estimator has not been applied to an experimental syndrome record.

The refined-physics code path is validated by the anchor tests and by end-to-end agreement with
the plain model, but is not verified byte-for-byte against the audited reference circuit builder,
which does not implement those effects.

Finally, the decoding question of Section~\ref{sec:res-impact} is answered for one strategy at
one operating point. The measured difference is consistently negative across independent runs but
is not individually significant, and we do not claim that knowing $\eta$ degrades decoding, only
that this strategy does not benefit from it.

\subsection{Explicit Non-Claims}
\label{sec:disc-nonclaims}

We do not claim that using raw stabilizer outcomes rather than detector differences is a novel
input representation. That choice is established for leakage detection in superconducting
devices~\cite{bultink2020repetitiveparity,varbanov2020leakagedetection} and has been applied to
atom loss by a learned decoder that consumes both raw outcomes and
detectors~\cite{wang2026aiqubitloss}. The contribution here is the estimand and the closed form:
a calibration-free estimator of the gate-pair loss correlation, together with the quantified
representation gap and the argument that explains it.

We do not claim any advantage over herald-based correlated-loss decoders. Those methods solve a
different problem with additional hardware.

We do not claim experimental validation. Every result is simulation, checked against known
answers rather than against measured data.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:disc-future}

Three directions follow directly. The first is applying the estimator to an experimental
syndrome record from a neutral-atom device, which requires no new theory and would convert the
present result from a method into a measurement. The second is integrating an estimated $\eta$
into a herald-based decoder with superstabilizer construction, which is the natural test of
whether the parameter has decoding value when used the way
Reference~\cite{perrin2026correlatedloss} uses it. The third is recovering the gate substep of a
loss event rather than averaging over candidate partners; the substep is encoded in the mixture
of partner outcomes, and exploiting it would tighten $\sigma_\eta$ by roughly a factor of four at
fixed shot count.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Correlated atom loss is a hardware property with published quantitative consequences for
error-correction performance, and until now it has been a parameter that studies vary rather
than measure. We have shown that it can be recovered in closed form from the measurement record
a surface-code memory experiment already produces, using two constants fixed by the code
structure and the readout mechanism rather than by calibration, and requiring no loss-resolving
readout, no loss-detection circuitry, and no trained model.

The estimator recovers the correlation within confidence intervals across its full range, on
three code families and three code distances, under a noise model that includes biased errors on
surviving atoms and a measured gate error budget. It reaches a standard error of $0.010$ at
$10^{6}$ shots when paired with a loss localizer of currently published quality, and $0.023$
with a deliberately poor one, so it does not depend on a strong loss decoder to be useful.

We also report that supplying the correlation to a decoder that discounts syndrome bits from
co-lost ancillas does not reduce the logical error rate, and that the reason is the same physics
the estimator relies on: a dead ancilla's reading is deterministic and therefore informative, so
treating it as erased discards more than it recovers. Whether the parameter improves decoding
when used inside a herald-based loss graph remains open.

The immediate application is characterization. A neutral-atom device can now report its
gate-pair loss correlation alongside its gate fidelity, and thereby determine which point on the
published threshold curves it occupies.
